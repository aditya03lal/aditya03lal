● INDIAN ECONOMY

• History
— Pre Liberalization: Focus on public sector development (steel, machinery), Nationalization of core sectors (banking, coal).
— Average growth of 3.5 % (While Singapore and Hong Kong boasted 7 %, overtaking even the UK in GDP per capita).
— 1991 Liberalization Triggers: Rajiv Gandhi assassination, USSR collapse, foreign exchange crisis, IMF pressure.
— Under PV Narasimha Rao and Manmohan Singh (Chaotic but transformative, we had no figure like Thatcher, Reagan or Xiaoping).
— Stabilization period + Setbacks (97-03): industrial decline, droughts, recessions, 6-7 % average growth since then.

• Current Performance
— Q2 FY26: GDP jumped 8.2 %, defying global slowdown. Strong growth in manufacturing, services, and investment.
— Growth averaging 7.85 % over past 2 years, debunks "deflator washout hypothesis" (that low inflation is artificially inflating growth).
— ICOR (Incremental Capital-Output Ratio) improving. 8.2 % growth with only 34 % investment (ICOR of 5 would require 40 %).
— Increase in productivity + Stable prices with no inflation i.e. This growth is real and sustainable.
— Trade deficit doubled (Good sign: Sharp increase in capital goods imports).

— Growth Drivers: GST and tax reforms reducing logistical frictions + Public Capex (Roads, Ports, Railways) + Fiscal Discipline.
— Foreign Investment: 1,600+ MNCs opening Global GCCs in design, R&D, and AI (creating spillover effects). Enabling 100+ unicorns.
— Defence production, space, and nuclear power opened to private players (Though renewable energy and storage advancing faster + cheaper).
— Nuclear concerns: High capital costs, Overruns (See France), Committing to massive targets (100 GW by 2047), Waste Management, Public Opposition.

• Union Budget 26/27
— Record spending on infrastructure: 7 high-speed rail corridors planned + 20 national waterways development.
— Foreign Investments: Loosened Equity Investment Limits (PROI) + Sector-Specific Tax Holidays and Exemptions + Regulatory Simplifications (CIS-cargo).
— Tax holiday for foreign-owned data centers (Till 2047), 5-year income tax exemption for non-residents supplying capital goods.
— Domestic: ₹ 10K cr. for MSME Champions Scheme + ₹10K cr. for BioPharma Shakti Scheme (Biologics/Pharma) + SHE Marts for women entrepreneurs.
— NRIs: Tax Collected at Source (TCS) reduced from 5% to 2% (Overseas remittances), 6-month foreign asset disclosure scheme to avoid prosecution.

● FOREIGN TRADE

• India-Europe FTA
— Preferential access to 99.5 % of Indian exports (zero duty for textiles, apparel, leather, jewelry). India to drop tariffs on automobiles, alcohol, premium foods.
— EU pledge $100 billion FDI over next 15 years + Creation of 1 million jobs, Frameworks supporting student, professional, and business mobility.
— India made legally binding commitments on labor, gender, and environmental standards for sustainable development. Concerns ⌄⌄
— Europe gets a say in domestic laws and economic regulations, for emerging sectors like AI. (Pharmaceutical industry: FTA reinforces voluntary licensing).
— EU carbon tax expected to reduce global emissions by only 0.1 % while affecting Indian agricultural commodity exports.
— Both parties to drop tariffs on green goods (May constrain domestic growth).

• India-USA Interim Trade Deal
— India drops tariffs on strategic industries: agricultural machinery, healthcare equipment, AI hardware. Tariffs reduced for alcohol and tree nuts.
— US reciprocates by dropping tariffs from 50% to 18% (0% on pharmaceuticals, precious stones, aircraft parts). India still protecting staple crops, dairy, fresh produce.
— Lower duty on apples and cotton, in specified quantities + India to increase imports in crude and petroleum products (Overall target: $500 B across 5 years).

# Trump Tariff Policy

## Reasons for Tariffs

**Policy Objectives**
- Protect strategic industries
- Negotiate leverage with China
- Reduce trade deficits

**Historical Context: US Manufacturing Offshoring**

*Phase 1: Japan (1960s-1980s)*
- US offloaded manufacturing to post-WW2 Japan
- Sectors: cars, electronics, consumer goods
- Advantages: high quality at lower cost, efficient/lean manufacturing (Toyota system)

*Phase 2: China (1980s-Present)*
- Deng Xiaoping's economic reforms encouraged foreign investment
- Sectors: electronics, apparel, assembly, low-cost consumer goods
- China became "the world's factory"

**Impact on US Manufacturing**
- NAFTA (1994) and China's WTO entry (2001) made global supply chains favor China
- US lost a quarter of manufacturing jobs in the Rust Belt (industrial Midwest, including Chicago area)

**Political Appeal**
- Appeals to MAGA base and pluralist coalition

---

## Why Tariffs Don't Work / Way Forward

**Limited Reshoring Success**
- Germany example: trade surplus rose from -1.5% to +5.8% of GDP while factory employment fell from 20% to 16%
- Similar decline rate to Ohio
- Cause: automation and global supply chains (not just offshoring)
- Global manufacturing's GDP share has stagnated post-2009 compared to services sector growth

**Flawed Economic Logic**
- Dean Baker's critique: "The tariff formula is like a doctor dividing your birthday by your height and prescribing medicine based on that"

**Better Approaches**

*Sustainable Trade Reform*
- Target trade imbalances that drive economic tension and instability
- Promote global cooperation and fluid world order

*Reframe Trade Deficits*
- Trade deficits aren't inherently bad
- They reflect foreign capital inflows supporting domestic investment and fiscal expansion

*Strategic Recommendations*
- Focus on reshoring critical industries (not all manufacturing)
- Pursue economic diversification
- Invest in workforce development
- Raise global wages (low wages create competitive edges that drive global imbalances)

# USSR Falldown

**Political & Administrative Failures**
- Bureaucratic corruption and inefficiencies
- Economic and political centralization
- Red tapism (excessive bureaucracy)
- Republics like Kazakhstan demanding decentralization due to resource exploitation

**Economic Collapse**
- Lack of innovation due to political oppression
- Shortages of commodities and consumer goods
- Centrally controlled demand and supply (command economy failures)
- Economic system unsustainable despite strong social provisions

**Social Programs (Note)**
- Housing, healthcare, and education were provided by the state
- Socioeconomic security was strong, but couldn't compensate for economic dysfunction

# China's Economic Growth

**Reform Era (1978-Present)**
- Deng Xiaoping's "Reform and Opening Up" policy (1978)
- Special Economic Zones attracted foreign investment
- Gradual market liberalization while maintaining political control

**Manufacturing Powerhouse**
- Became "world's factory" through low-cost labor and infrastructure investment
- WTO entry (2001) accelerated integration into global supply chains
- Massive urbanization and industrialization lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty

**State-Directed Capitalism**
- Hybrid model: market mechanisms with state guidance
- Strategic industrial policy and five-year plans
- Heavy investment in infrastructure, technology, and education

**Current Position**
- Second-largest economy globally
- Moving up value chain from assembly to innovation (EVs, renewables, AI)
- Belt and Road Initiative extending economic influence globally

# BRICS

**Overview**
- Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa
- Expanded 2024: Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, UAE added
- Represents major emerging economies and Global South

**Core Objectives**
- Reduce dependence on Western-dominated financial systems (de-dollarization)
- New Development Bank (2014) as alternative to IMF/World Bank
- Push for multipolar world order and reform of global institutions
- Greater representation for developing nations in global governance

**BRICS Chair 2026: India**
- Annual rotating chairmanship
- Expected priorities: digital infrastructure, BRICS payment systems, counter-terrorism, reform of multilateral institutions
- India's balancing act: managing China tensions while cooperating, maintaining strategic autonomy (Quad vs BRICS)
- Key challenge: managing diverse interests among expanded membership with competing geopolitical priorities
